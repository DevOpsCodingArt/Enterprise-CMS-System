Most UXUI advice out there teaches you the same surface level stuff. Use white space, align your text, pick better colors. But if you want to actually become a great designer, the kind who creates interfaces people love to use and remember, you need more than that. In this video, we'll be sharing five advanced UXUI design tips you won't hear in most courses or tutorials. Let's get

into it. One, personalize the experience based on user behavior. When you're designing a product, it's important to remember that not all users are the same. Some are brand new, some are coming back after a few uses, and others are deeply engaged. But what many beginner designers do is show everyone the same screen, no matter where they are in their journey. That's a missed

opportunity. Designing experiences that adapt to different types of users can make your products feel smarter, more personal, and way more useful. Let me show you what this looks like using a fitness app. The first screen is for a new user. They've just signed up, so the app keeps things really simple. burned. Now, here's a super user, someone who's fully engaged and committed to their fitness goals. The app experience becomes more advanced. We start with useful stats like daily steps, calories burned, and heart rate. Then it moves into their workout plan and personalized diet suggestions. At this stage, it's not about helping them get started. It's about helping them

Instead of creating one generic experience for everyone, think about how your interface can adapt to what the user needs right now. Small changes like personalized messages, tailored content, or progress tracking can make your product feel more human, more helpful, and more valuable.

Two, design smarter search. Search is one of the most critical features in any product. But it's also one of the most overlooked in design. Most designers just drop in a search bar and move on. But good search design isn't just about function. It's about how it feels, how fast it helps users get what they need, and how much effort it saves them. Think

Now, let's look at a smarter version of the same screen. The search bar is still there, ready to use, but underneath it, the interface offers subtle, useful suggestions. Things like recent searches, so the user can quickly pick

up where they left off. Popular items, which gives them a sense of what others are finding useful. Personalized recommendations based on their past behavior or interest. friction and give them somewhere to start. Three, smarter order tracking design. Here's something a lot of designers overlook. Post-purchase experiences matter just as much as the purchase itself. From the very first glance, this screen makes you feel like everything is under control. It opens with a confident message. Your order is on the way, paired with clear delivery details and visual icons that help

communicate the time window and address instantly. The courier section is no longer just a name and number. It's human. There's a photo, a name, and quick access buttons to call or message, which not only adds trust, but makes the experience feel more personal and responsive. The items, no more long text lists. And one of the best touches, the order history is now a visual timeline.

The first version is very basic, just a list of category names and C selection links. There's nothing wrong with it. It's clean and minimal. But the problem is it

puts all the effort on the user. Every line looks the same. There's no visual rhythm, no hierarchy, and no cues that help guide the eye. You have to read through every label to find what you want. Now, the second version is something we see a lot from junior designers. It looks more designed and might even seem impressive at first glance, but here's the thing. It focuses

Now, let's compare that to logging your food every day to track calories or macros. This is where sliders and scroll wheels break down. They're too slow, too fiddly, and don't offer the kind of control users need. If you want to log food quantity like 350 g, I don't want to scroll endlessly or try to drag a slider to the perfect number. Text fields are better here because they're

faster, more precise, and less frustrating when used repeatedly. So, how do you choose? Here is a simple rule of thumb. Use sliders or scroll wheels for casual one-time inputs where precision isn't critical. Use text fields, steppers, or number inputs for frequent, precise, or repetitive tasks.
